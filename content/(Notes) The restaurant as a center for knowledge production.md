Over the last 10 years, I’ve lived in communes, ecovillages and co-living spaces, studying how coherence and trust form. Across them all, I saw a recurring pattern: dining together is the single most reliable entry point for bringing strangers into community.  
After many years of community projects, the path seemed obvious – what if we port this pattern to the city? What if a restaurant could rebuild our eroding social and civic infra?  
One of my hopes for the restaurant, then, is that it introduces and inaugurates a new possible “form” of the restaurant, one that is both more profitable and meaningful. If this becomes a part of the ontology of restaurants, it can scale through others, in much the way that “farm-to-table” scaled not through a single operator, but through the proliferation of an idea.  
In a more abstract sense, the restaurant doubles as a “storefront lab”: a prototype for new civic primitives. Most new city projects focus on the macro: zoning, urbanist master plans, and governance. But few interrogate the microstructure – the storefronts and ground-level commercial spaces through which civic life is lived and reproduced. We’re interested in the latter: exploring how the fundamental units of a city can themselves be reimagined as vehicles for learning, belonging, and coordination. I'm conceiving of the restaurant as a composable element of better neighborhoods and cities.  
To achieve this, Garnet must be a place of knowledge production – a restaurant that learns in public and produces open-source documentation, patterns, and tools that others can adopt. Our own “pattern language”. I want to ensure we don’t just rebuild the same commercial typologies and social institutions, but generate new ones capable of sustaining emergent civic life.  

This effort also works on another fronts. The publishing becomes a way for curious individuals to learn more about the restaurant, and to orient their attention before they come in for dinner. Most restaurants just publish their menu and aesthetics, but we want to publish our ideas. For the typical diner, we’ll have more digestible branding and language, but for the curious diner, it becomes a unique invitation to dine and imagine with us.

## **The Restaurant as Research Organism**

### I. The Street as Laboratory

Cities are systems of relationship. They are made and remade each day through the rhythms of encounter — a nod between neighbors, a pause in front of a storefront, the faint murmur of conversation from an open window.

Jane Jacobs understood this deeply. Her work reminds us that real urban knowledge does not emerge from planning offices or academic departments but from the eyes and hands of the people who live and work at street level. Shopkeepers, in her view, were both witnesses and stewards — the ones most attuned to the subtle ecology of a block.

A restaurant, then, inherits this role. It is a node of attention, positioned where private lives touch the public realm. To operate a restaurant is to observe the city, not abstractly but through the lived gestures of its inhabitants: how they enter, linger, listen, and belong.

---

### II. The Restaurant as Site of Knowledge

The restaurant is one of the few remaining civic rooms where strangers regularly gather. It is both intimate and public, bounded and porous. Beneath the surface of service, it performs a deeper social function — it stages the act of _being together_.

Yet restaurants rarely think of themselves as places that _learn_. The conventional model is didactic: the kitchen speaks, the guest receives. Information flows one way, and whatever knowledge is generated — about how people connect, how conversation flows, how mood shifts with light and sound — tends to dissipate each night.

But what if the restaurant were understood as a form of inquiry? What if its daily operations — seating, serving, observing, reflecting — were treated as methods of research? The dining room could become a kind of Harkness table for the city: circular, participatory, alive to dialogue rather than performance.

In most spaces, we inhabit that space with others – a cafe, a street, a subway, a housewarming, or even an apartment with roommates. And in each of these settings, there exist norms – those shared, but often unarticulated, rules – which guide and constrain our actions in relation to those people. Most places that call themselves “third spaces” simply provide the physical space, but not the social infrastructure. I believe the basis of great cities is people interacting. The restaurant should not only be a place where people say hello, but where they get to know each other, and build the deep ties that sustain our lives. To this end, we need to construct a space that invites relating, which requires the creation of new pro-social norms.


To share a meal is to share attention. Commensality is not only social but epistemic — a way of knowing through proximity, rhythm, and care. Around the table, knowledge is generated in the pauses, the overlaps, the small negotiations of etiquette and empathy.

This knowledge is rarely written down. It resides in tacit forms: gestures of welcome, tone of voice, the pacing of conversation. Yet these micro-patterns reveal how trust forms and how belonging takes shape.

The restaurant, by observing these dynamics over time, becomes a research organism — sensing, recording, and interpreting the continuous experiment of human gathering. Every meal becomes a data point, every service a field study in the texture of community.

**# The Community CRM

The restaurant will have a CRM, and it will, I believe, serve as a significant tool towards the end of social infrastructure. Unlike a traditional Customer oriented system, this will be a Community relationship management system. Why is this important? Two reasons

  

The Traditional Reason

  

The owners and staff of spaces learn so much about their customers. They see the papers they read, the attitudes they hold, and glimpse into their interests and curiosities from conversations. These are rarely tracked and documented, but the spaces we, humans, tend to enjoy and patronize, over and over, have employees who remember these small details. 

  

It is the remembering of a person, the acknowledgment of that memory, and the expressed contentment in seeing that person again that creates feelings of warmth and intimacy. It is these feelings which people want, and which inform their movements through a city of unending storefronts. It is a mistake to think that these feelings are principally generated through interior design decisions. Those decisions surely matter, too, and inform one’s feelings and movements, but it is authentic human interaction which conveys the feelings of intimacy and warmth in a storefront space. 

  

Think about sitting with a group of friends on the floor of an unfurnished apartment, backs hunched over takeout containers and plastic utensils. These feelings are transmitted between people mostly, and secondarily between people and space (or people and X, Y, Z). 

  

At the cafe I mentioned earlier, Nako, the space had the coldness of an AI-generated coffee shop. Yet, the owner, Erik, took the moment, before asking what I would like to order, to ask how I was. I answered, and asked him too. Then, he asked what I’d like to order. The micro-interaction unfolded at the pace of a human interaction, not a commoditized one. The space was, for my tastes and in isolation, close to awful, but I sat there for several hours and felt it to be intimate and warm. 

  

It is for this reason that you have platforms, like that which Maggie Tang is working on ([Loyalist](https://www.loyalist.com/)), dedicated to assisting restaurants in this work. 

  

So, part of this is: Can the storefront have an excellent memory? Can a more casual spot have the attentiveness, without the pretension, of a place like Eleven Madison Park? I very much think so. 

  

Now, it is necessary that the CRM contributes to authentic human relationships. The CRM is there to aid the restaurant in knowing and talking with its 100s of diners, as if they were friends coming over to our home for dinner. It is not there to make blind and depersonalized requests in their inbox, asking them to “come back for a fourth time, and get 25% off a drink of $6 or less.” It should serve as an extension of our memory, an extension of our humanity. 

  

Now, to the more compelling reason… To Introduce:

  

Until now, we’ve discussed a number of potential methods of and settings for human-to-human interaction. One attempt is to have strangers sit around a table, over many dinners. Another, is to set a filter – an interest, a life stage, a spiritual orientation, etc… Another is to set new norms of interaction, and let people choose who they wish to engage with. And others…

  

Yet, another undiscussed method for the restaurant – a restaurant that has as its primary design consideration to enable real and meaningful connection – is to serve as matchmaker. 

  

When you sign-up to join us for a meal, we’ll ask for some information. Their basic information of course, like their name and email, and also a question that will look something like this… 

  

Would you like us to introduce you to others who we think you’d get along with? It is never automated, we only make personal introductions, and we’ll only do so if we feel strongly about the choice. We also only introduce people when they’ve joined us for a meal before, so we ensure they are real people! Presently, we only introduce people who are near the restaurant – we want to help build a more connected neighborhood. If you are interested, just let us know a bit more about yourself!

  

If they select yes, they’ll be prompted to finish their reservation and told that they’ll receive an email from us after with a few questions. This will be a simple email automation, and requires no elaborate setup. 

  

The questions we ask, the style of introduction, the cadence of sending introductions as a team, the method of choosing two, or more, people to be introduced, the possible incentives for meeting in the restaurant or having recurring interaction… These are some of the considerations I’ve extensively engaged with in past work and which we’ll later play around with for the restaurant. 

  

The CRM will hold these entries and serve as our extended brain to make connections across entries. The CRM will allow us to play neighborhood matchmaker, not as a public facing value-proposition, but as one among many background techniques for crafting an interconnected and rich relational fabric. In addition to the analogue yearbook in the space, we’ll have several avenues for customer and employee generated new interaction.**

### IV. The Nature of the Knowledge

The kind of knowledge a restaurant produces cannot be standardized or abstracted. It is **situated** (Donna Haraway), **tacit** (Michael Polanyi), and **experiential** (John Dewey).

It emerges from context: from the neighborhood’s particular social fabric, from the sensory cues of the space, from the intentions of the hosts and the moods of the guests. It is local, embodied, and constantly evolving.

Because of this, it must be documented differently. Rather than reports or metrics, it may take the form of:

- communal journals,
    
- annotated floor plans mapping how people move or cluster,
    
- reflections from staff and guests,
    
- or even design prototypes inspired by observed social patterns.
    

Through accumulation, these fragments form a living archive — a body of urban ethnography grounded in practice.

### V. Participation and Reciprocity

If the restaurant is a research organism, then its participants are not subjects but collaborators. Knowledge is co-created through reciprocal awareness: the staff’s attentiveness to guests, guests’ attentiveness to one another, and the restaurant’s attentiveness to itself.

The role of the restaurant becomes one of facilitation rather than control — designing conditions that allow consciousness to emerge. Spatial cues, rituals, and pacing all serve as invitations to awareness. The service itself becomes an experiment in distributed intelligence: how does a group of strangers collectively produce comfort, curiosity, and connection?

This shifts the ethic of hospitality. It is no longer about perfection of service, but about cultivation of understanding — the shared inquiry into how we inhabit space and one another’s company.




---

### VI. Toward a Pattern Language of Gathering

Over time, the insights from such a restaurant could form the basis for a new **pattern language** — a vocabulary of spatial and social forms that enable belonging.

These patterns might describe the optimal size of a table for spontaneous conversation, or the rhythm by which new guests are absorbed into a regular group, or the ways lighting and sound modulate vulnerability.

Just as Christopher Alexander proposed a pattern language for architecture, a restaurant attentive to its own dynamics could propose one for _convivial architecture_ — a way of designing spaces that make community more likely.

Such knowledge, shared openly, would return to the city: a gift from practice to theory, from the lived to the abstract, from the restaurant back to the street.

---

### VII. The Conscious Restaurant

To call a restaurant _conscious_ is to recognize it as a living system — one that perceives, learns, and adapts. A conscious restaurant is aware of its influence on the civic life around it and intentional in how it shapes that life.

It knows that hospitality is not simply service but social infrastructure. It understands that each meal is a civic act — an experiment in belonging conducted through taste, touch, and time.

In this sense, the conscious restaurant is both organism and observer, host and historian. It nourishes not only the body but the city’s capacity for understanding itself.

---

### VIII. Conclusion

We are accustomed to thinking of knowledge as the domain of universities and think tanks — places where the world is studied from a distance. But there are other sites of inquiry, quieter and closer to daily life. The street corner. The stoop. The restaurant table.

If we can learn to treat these spaces as sources of insight — if we can recognize in the act of gathering a form of research — then we may begin to rebuild our civic life on more human terms.

The restaurant, at its highest form, is not merely a place to eat. It is a research organism embedded in the living tissue of the city, generating knowledge about how people come to know one another — and, through one another, to know the world.

**A broader question for me is:** how can the restaurant help prototype and document the development of new civic primitives? Most new city projects focus on the macro: zoning, urbanist master plans, governance. But few interrogate the microstructure – the storefronts and tables through which civic life is actually lived and reproduced. Through the restaurant, I want to explore how the fundamental units of a city can be reimagined as vehicles for learning, belonging, and coordination. New forms of _the storefront_ should be part of our civic toolkit, a composable element of city infrastructure. I want to ensure we don’t just rebuild the same commercial typologies and social institutions, but generate new ones capable of enabling emergent civic life.


Part of this is motivated by belief, and other exploration. I believe that neighborliness, love of place, and deep ties with people are the foundational and necessary features by which we improve the place we live. I think storefronts can play a critical role in the development of those features. And I wish to explore how it does so, and what exactly its role should be, so that I may come to think more abstractly, and yet practically, about what I and we can do to improve the places we live and how we live.

## I. Framing: Why a Restaurant Can Be More Than a Restaurant

The restaurant is one of the city’s most important civic interfaces — a storefront that gathers, observes, and learns. Traditionally, it’s understood as a site of service or hospitality. But what if it could also be a **research organism** — a place that studies itself and its environment in order to deepen civic and social understanding?

- The storefront as a **fundamental civic unit** — where public life, culture, and knowledge are reproduced.
    
- Inspired by Jane Jacobs: the shopkeeper as _observer_ of the street. Here, the shopkeeper becomes _researcher_.
    
- The restaurant as a **conscious storefront** — aware of its rhythms, its community, and its capacity to shape social life.
    
- The question: _Can the restaurant be a site of knowledge production?_
    
- The civic thesis: _What if storefronts were as epistemically valuable as universities or labs?_
    
- In a time of urban loneliness, the restaurant can act as a **social infrastructure** for belonging and meaning.
    

---

## II. Forms of Knowledge

The knowledge a restaurant produces is not academic but _situated_, _tactile_, and _relational_. It emerges through repetition, craft, and care — an embodied form of research.

- “Storefront knowledge” vs. “academic knowledge.” The former is lived, sensory, iterative.
    
- The restaurant as a **biodynamic system** — people, tools, and space form an interdependent ecology.
    
- The restaurant learns through patterns: who comes, what conversations form, what gestures invite ease.
    
- Knowledge here is compost: slow, cumulative, and grounded in daily practice.
    
- Documentation practices — notebooks, communal journals, guest reflections — formalize the growth of this knowledge.
    
- The space becomes both a **studio for the chef** and a **collective organism** that learns with others.
    
- Guests, staff, and neighbors become contributors to a shared body of civic learning.
    
- “Feedback Beyond Yelp” — reflection replaces rating; participation replaces consumption.
    

---

## III. Methods: How Knowledge Is Produced

Learning happens through cycles of attention, reflection, and reintegration — the same iterative rhythm as any studio practice.

- Every meal, conversation, and event is a small social study: _what fosters connection?_
    
- Hosting becomes an act of inquiry. Each evening generates insight into human behavior and belonging.
    
- Replace transactional feedback with **dialogical reflection** — guests co-produce understanding.
    
- The restaurant operates like a **field site** — exploring how design, ritual, and food shape social connection.
    
- Observation → documentation → synthesis → reintegration into practice.
    
- The dining room itself is a **test kitchen for human connection.**
    
- “Questions are more interesting than answers.” The space frames questions through experience.
    
- Each iteration makes the restaurant more conscious of its civic and emotional effects.
    

---

## IV. Pedagogy and Civic Learning

The restaurant functions as a civic classroom — a place where people learn not through lectures, but through shared experience.

- The pedagogy of the table: sensory, relational, and embodied.
    
- Guests, staff, and community members are co-learners in an ongoing studio.
    
- “Learning through hosting” — service as a form of observation and study.
    
- The restaurant continues the lineage of _studio-based learning_ — like architecture schools, but for civic life.
    
- It offers a secular, civic form of education: reflection, friendship, curiosity.
    
- As biodynamic farms teach ecological interdependence, the restaurant teaches social interdependence.
    
- “Invest less in careers, more in studios.” The restaurant becomes a lifelong studio for civic education.
    
- Outputs — zines, notebooks, public reflections — share knowledge beyond the space itself.
    

---

## V. Design and Infrastructure

Design is not aesthetic alone — it’s behavioral. Each spatial and temporal element shapes the possibility of attention, ease, and connection.

- Every design decision is a **social experiment**: how light, sound, or pacing shape belonging.
    
- The space must invite participation, not performance — curiosity, not spectacle.
    
- Relationship gradients: from stranger → acquaintance → regular → friend → collaborator.
    
- Rituals, prompts, and spatial cues help guide these transitions naturally.
    
- The restaurant doubles as a **collective studio** — with physical and social infrastructure for reflection.
    
- Borrowing from Mugaritz, create **seasons of experimentation** — cycles of testing, rest, and renewal.
    
- Changes are documented and intentional — the space learns as it evolves.
    
- Feedback loops are built into the architecture itself — analog and emotional sensors of engagement.
    
- The result is a **conscious restaurant**: a space aware of its own patterns of connection.

## VII. Personal Orientation / Motivation

The restaurant is both an external project and an internal one — the culmination of your own study, curiosity, and search for grounded forms of meaning.

- Draws from years of studying communes, ecovillages, and social learning environments.
    
- A turn from abstraction toward tactile, embodied ways of knowing.
    
- A place to practice values of friendship, curiosity, and attention.
    
- “Friendships matter most. I’m doing this for me too.”
    
- Food as _poetry_: an ephemeral but profound form of meaning.
    
- Cooking as _tactile wisdom_: a form of thinking through doing.
    
- This is both a studio and a mirror — an ongoing inquiry into life, city, and self.
    

---

## VIII. Goals and Civic Implications

The ambition extends beyond a single restaurant. The restaurant becomes a prototype — a small piece of civic infrastructure capable of producing new forms of urban knowledge and belonging.

- Build convivial cityscapes starting from convivial foodscapes.
    
- The restaurant as a **test storefront** for civic innovation.
    
- Develop new civic primitives at the micro scale: table, block, storefront.
    
- Over time, network these micro-institutions into a distributed civic learning ecosystem.
    
- Produce open-source documentation: a _pattern language for social infrastructure_.
    
- Rethink policy, investment, and zoning to support **storefronts that learn**.
    
- Shift from career-centric to **studio-centric** models of contribution and meaning.
    
- “A restaurant that learns” is a metaphor — and prototype — for **a city that learns.**
