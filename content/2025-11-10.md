When I was thinking of opening a restaurant where strangers ate together, I didn't initially think of it as a more profitable model. 

I was studying the development of relationships, social coherence and trust in communities and thinking about new forms of urban social infrastructure. 



I saw this pattern across co-living, communes, school... where eating with strangers was a remarkably powerful social force. And my research in commensality backed this up. And I was thinking about the decline in social institutions, the rise in solo dining, the decline in civic engagement... and I thought, what if a restaurant could get neighbors and strangers eating together. If we know more people where we live, we stay longer, we're happier, more partnerships sprout, we invest in our local area, and a long list of other things. Essentially, I viewed the simple act of eating together as having the posiblity of begetting so many of ht eother good thigns. I cared about building better cities, but most of the city folks were new urbanist prinicples, public trasnit nerds, or building better cities. I was curious by the questions of introducing new social forms of the restaurant, and storefornt generally – operating as a storefront labs (much like sidewalk labs) to invent new civic primatives. 

In a more abstract sense, the restaurant doubles as a “storefront lab”: a prototype for new civic primitives. Most new city projects focus on the macro: zoning, urbanist master plans, and governance. But few interrogate the microstructure – the storefronts and ground-level commercial spaces through which civic life is lived and reproduced. We’re interested in the latter: exploring how the fundamental units of a city can themselves be reimagined as vehicles for learning, belonging, and coordination. I'm conceiving of the restaurant as a composable element of better neighborhoods and cities.
