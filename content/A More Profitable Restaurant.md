# Restaurants and Their Problems

I've worked in restaurants, in both the FOH, BOH and in an administrative capacity. 

The restaurant industry is hard to build in. It is plagued by a number of major problems, which make profitability and sustainability hard-earned, and rare. Let me discuss, briefly, what these problems are. 

## Efficiency

  

There are a collection of problems I'm gonna bucket as "efficiency" problems. 

**Bleedout**

Restaurants typically have mixed table-sizes, which may track to a 2-top, a 4-top, and so on. Let's say your seating capacity is 50 people across 15 tables. At a given time, you may only have 35 people across those 15 tables (For example, you may have 1 person at a 2-top, or 3 at a 4-top). In a practical sense, you are *full* – you cannot accommodate anyone else. Yet, you are only operating at 70% efficiency – 15 seats are empty. We can call this bleedout. 

**Turning Tables**

In a restaurant, the same table can be used accross multiple parties over a single service. That is, if a party sits down at 5:30, we could also book a 7:30 reservation at the same table (or seat a walk-in at that time). So a 4-top could potentially accommodate 8 people over a 4-hour service window, or even 12. However, given inefficiencies in timing, often we don't get to turn that table more than once. Maybe the first seating at that 4-top is at 7:00, and you won't be able to accommodate a group of 4 who inquire about an 8:00pm sitting. If the two groups were at 6:00 and 8:30, that'd be great, but it didn't line up that way. So, that one 4-top that can sit 8 over the course of a night, now only sat 4. For that table, we're just operating at 50% efficiency. 

In a city like New York, this especially matters, because to survive you have to be busy. Assuming your busy, the question becomes: how efficient is the restaurant during these hours? 


Even in a quiet Northwest CT town like West Cornwall, our Friday-Sunday services typically have at least 15-30 unique parties on the waitlist. If we were more efficient, we without doubt could accommodate most of these parties. Our average check size (per person) is about $83. If we miss out on 15 parties a night, with an average party size of 2, we're missing out on $2490/night in revenue. That is $7470/week (just accounting for Friday-Sunday), roughly $30,000/month, and roughly $360,000/year. 

**Sqft Usage**

In a typical restaurant, a dining room is made up of a mix of two tops and four tops, with composable tables for larger parties. As people have fewer close friends and solo-dining has risen dramatically, restaurants are offering even more small # table sizes. Why does this matter? Well, each table must be spaced out from the one next to it. The space that sits in between these tables is not revenue generating space, so we can consider it dead space. That is a restaurant may have 1000 sq ft of dining room, but only 300 sqft of usable space, which is the revenue generating portion of that space. Generally speaking, the more standalone tables, the more dead space. Private

Labor

  

I asked Gabe McMackin, the chef I work with at the Pink House, and at an earlier time his Michelin starred the Finch what the most difficult problem in his restaurants were. Without hesitation he said labor.

Labor is, without a near competitor, the most important consideration, the most irritatingly difficult, and the most expensive component in operating a restaurant. 

In conventional restaurants, labor is the biggest expense, by a pretty wide margin. Let's look, for example, at Atlas Food Group, which owns 50 restaurant/bar concepts. They're slated to do about $200 million in revenue this year. Of that, $46.5M is in labor costs alone. The next closest line item is food costs at $25M and occupancy costs at $9.4M. Their labor costs are about 5x what their occupancy costs are. And food Groups, like Atlas, are able to function much more efficiently than independent establishments. If you look at the 2019 P&L statements of Mei Mei, an independently run and popular Boston restaurant (in the late 2010’s in Boston), they spent 44.9% on labor and another 9.5% on administrative payroll – about 59% in total. This is more typical of a small and independently run establishment. 

Restaurant employees – and specifically FOH employees – are hard to find, schedule, retain, etc... Most often, servers will serve as a means to make money for some more primary pursuit of theirs. Their job as a server is not the primary interest, and so building culture and a sense of pride in their work is difficult. They are transient, so as a GM you are consistently looking for and onboarding staff. As they have other pursuits, scheduling FOH is time-consuming and tricky. Not only is the labor attributable to the greatest cost, but also a great time sync - interviewing, managing, scheduling, payroll, etc.. 

  


The other problem with labor, in the restaurant industry, is that assigns people's formal roles, especially in the restaurant industry – servers, server assistants, runners, garde manger, sous, host, etc.. Yet, there are a slew of inefficiencies when roles are carried out like this. For one, each person thinks of themselves in their role, and natural status hierarchies develop, internal and external to the space. These are highly counterproductive to building an exceptional experience, because those lower on the list feel little ownership in the experience, and for those higher up, their ego gets in the way. 

  

At a business level, what also happens is that you have a lot of people standing around... As a server, one spends most of my time scanning the room – there is no food to run, no plates to clear, no orders to take. So, you wander the dining room to pay attention to the status of diners. Because there are defined roles and because of American hospitality ideals, there isn't much else to do. It's a waste of money and time.

  But, why do you need so much labor?

  

A major reason you need so much labor is that each table is treated like its own world. So, running a conventional restaurant requires that you attend to the specific affairs of each table – each person is brought through the journey of their meal at a different pace. So you have to cater to dozens of different worlds, simultaneously. You need so much labor because, every day, you are building lots of little worlds. 

  
Another reason is that most restaurants have a long menu of options, so you need a large BOH team to prep and execute that menu. Fine dining restaurants may make this a little easier on themselves, by offering tasting menus or pre-set seating times. BUT, in these settings, there is even more work to create distinct and beautiful worlds for diners, so the labor costs are still very high, in both FOH and BOH

  

Of course, you also have other labor costs, like a host to greet people, a general manager, a cashier to take orders, or the like (depending on the concept).

# Commensal Hospitality



Pretty basic. Come in at set time. Fill seats




When I speak here of conventional hospitality, I’m referring to the classic hospitality you’d get in a moderately upscale NYC establishment.

Conventional hospitality caters to each table individually, to make their dining experience a little world unto itself. Thus, the restaurant is a dozen little worlds, a universe. And to cater to that, you need a bunch of servers. Every person walking in wants to have a dining experience about them – the power dynamic is immediately understood, the diner is being _**served**._ In commensal hospitality (commensality, simply, is _the act of eating and drinking together_), the diners eat, drink and commune together – it is one large universe. Simply, it is dining together. There are surely variants of a hospitality model built on the idea of commensality, but this is our version.

What does this mean? It means that the object of the meal is not the _**being-served**_ and the _**feeling powerful and special**,_ it is the _**being with other people**_ and _**feeling human**_. The prior requires the server, someone to labor over, and for, you. The employee is subordinate to the diner. The latter only requires other diners – the principle relationship is diner to diner.

Commensal hospitality facilitates and curates a communal experience. It is more like a dinner party, or a supper club, than a typical restaurant. Functionally, this is a model of hospitality built for human gathering.

Restaurants, or cafes, may offer shared-tables but not _communal_ tables. If you go to one of the nearly 100 Benihana restaurants, or any other Hibachi restaurant, you’ll likely be seated with other people. These shared tables are mostly practical reasons – the hibachi chef needs a large enough grill, and the economics wouldn’t make sense for one chef to cook for only two or three people. You are intended to watch a performance, as you might in a theater – the object of attention is the chef, not the others around you. In many NY cafes, you’ll see large tables filled with people – throngs of the laptop-class. Again, these are practical considerations of the cafe to seat more people, but each person’s attention is towards the laptop. Restaurants, more and more, are offering communal tables… and they tend to be considered the worst seat in the house. Why? Because you are going out to feel _**special**_, and you want to be treated as a standalone _**world**_ – shared seating doesn’t offer this feeling.

At [The Pink House](https://www.thepinkhousect.com/), we had a gorgeous long table in the center of our dining room. It is intended as a flex table – it could be for a party of 8 or 10, or it could serve two smaller parties. When it is the latter, we put tall vases of flowers in the middle of the table to make it as minimally-shared as possible.

Shared seating sucks because it is still the **same conventional hospitality**. You not only need the physical proximity of sharing a table, but a communal experiential layer to bridge the social gap. Moreover, you need people coming in who **want** to have a communal experience, not those hoping to have a dinner with friends, only to be seated next to a bunch of schmucks.

<aside> 💡

**A quick note:** Modern American hospitality is relatively new, and the world of eating-out looks vastly different across time and place (great book [here](https://www.amazon.com/Dining-Out-Global-History-Restaurants/dp/1789140579) on the history of dining). Restaurants are not required to abide by certain standards of dining, but owners stick to these standards because they are familiar. Restaurants can be near anything their owners want them to be.

</aside>

The most important part of commensal hospitality is that we have **no servers**. Servers actively interfere with commensality. It is not only that you don’t need them, you don’t **want** them. Instead, an **Experience Designer**, alongside an operations assistant and the chefs, crafts an experience that facilitates horizontal interactions between diners. So, how do you handle a dining room without servers?

For one, we have set meal times, twice an evening. This supports and improves the operation on a number of fronts, but most importantly, it enables communal eating. The major job of the server is to manage multiple tables, each sat at a different time; if everyone is sat at the same time, they no longer have to do this managing.

What are the other main work-patterns for the server that we address?

1. **Greet Guests**
    1. With set meal times, guests are greeted as they walk on. Then, we greet the whole restaurant during each seating (ie. at 5:30 and at 8:00).
2. **Take Order**
    1. No ordering required. Each diner receives the same daily offering, and allergies are known ahead of the dinner.
3. **Bring out, and Clear, Food**
    1. The whole staff brings out, and clears, dishes at once. Since the whole dining room is served at once, chefs are freed up to be more involved in the dining room. Chefs, not servers, explain the dishes, and alongside the Experience Designer may help facilitate the communal experience.
    2. At times, we’ll invite guests to the kitchen to learn about a dish and help bring out and describe that dish to their fellow diners.
    3. We aren’t prissy with changing silverware or clearing crumbs - our attention is crafting an experience. Humanity > luxury.
4. **Survey the Dining Room for Guest’s Needs**
    1. Communal dining makes scanning the dining room much easier for us; it is one compact setting. Guests pour each other water, and a large chest in the dining room will have access to more napkins, and the like, for guests who need. We encourage guests to stand up and move around as if it were their living room – securing items themselves brings them into a feeling of ownership and enables them to serve others, which builds trust and kinship.
5. **Handle Payment**
    1. Typically, servers have to bring the check, run the check at the POS system, input the tip, etc.. Here, you pay for your meal ahead of time, with a set service charge, so there are no checks.
6. **Set and Reset Table**
    1. Before each seating, we set the dining room as a team.

Commensal Hospitality isn’t just stripping away – out with the servers and all is solved! No, commensal hospitality introduces, just as it removes. The restaurant is designed for the development and exploration of human to human relationships – those design considerations, and real-time decisions, are numerous, and we’ll need to hire incredible people to execute on it well.

**### Less Labor, Less $ Outlay

Labor is the biggest cost for a restaurant. Without servers, we’ve reduced the labor cost a few dozen %. We also don’t need a host/hostess. Without servers, we also don’t need a General Manager (GM). And, with set menus and no ticketing system, we also don’t need an Expeditor (Expo). Finally, with set seating times and communally-oriented food, we also need fewer chefs. 

  

We just removed servers, the GM, the host/hostess, the Expo, and some number of chefs. Even accounting for new hires – an Experience Designer and a Head of Operations (more here later) – that is, depending on the restaurant, about a 50% labor reduction. 

  

If your total labor spend is 45%, that is a 22.5% reduction in total spend. 

  

### Less Labor, Less Headache

Labor is considered the biggest headache in the restaurant industry, both for $ outlay and for time outlay.

  

With less labor, I’ve freed up much of my time. Great servers are difficult to hire, train, and retain. I can spend less of my time hiring, scheduling, managing conflict, organizing payroll, and more…  and instead focus on cooking, r&d, experience-design, and business-level considerations and goals. Too many restaurant owners are weighed down by the minutiae of a large team, so they direct all their time to staying afloat rather than the compounding effects of progressively improving the operation and experience. 

  

Of course, eliminating servers doesn’t only reduce the headache. Servers are engaged in tasks, through a daily opening checklist and side work during their shift – folding napkins, polishing silverware and glasses, organizing the server station, and the like. Certain work, though minimal, will have to get covered by the team before service, between services, and after services. 

  

### Less Labor, Higher Salaries

In most restaurants, servers have some of the highest annual income through tips. Chefs, dishwashers, and others meanwhile, have annual incomes that can be hard to live by. Since we have no servers, the “tip”, which will be in the form of a transparent 20% service charge, will be distributed across the whole team (Legally, tips can only go to service workers, not chefs, so to split the “tip”, it can’t be a formal tip left up to the diner). As we have a small team, each employee will make a total salary that is higher than almost any other restaurant in NYC. 

  

For example, let’s say a chef’s starting salary is $25/hr and we have a $25 dinner service charge and a $4 lunch service charge. If we serve 45 people for lunch, and 45 people nightly, that is $1305/daily in service charges. Split evenly across eight people, that is $163, which comes out to just over $20/hour. Now, that chef is making $93,600/yr salary. Moreover, we’ll be doing a 10% profit share annually (more information shared elsewhere in this document), which would add a projected $4062.5, for a $97,662.5/yr salary. 

  

For an experience-designer, who might be in a community-facing role at a technology company, we may well be able to match or exceed their salary, and give them a compensation package that will net out at over 6-figures. 

  

A small team means higher incomes for everyone.

### A Single Experience

Most restaurants manage two interfaces – the outdoors to the dining room, and the dining room to the kitchen. If a restaurant is open from 5-10pm, they must manage the progressive trickle of guests inside. Even if they offer reservations, they will still have new tables every half hour. This is the job of the host or hostess. 

  

Servers, then, manage the interface between the dining room and kitchen. An Expo is also involved in this interface. 

  

This is a complex setting, because you are, at once, timing a dozen different performances. The server is having to take the order of one table, bring out the appetizers to another, run the check of yet another, and so on. And the kitchen is mired in this mess too – they are cooking a dozen different courses at once, with attention to the unique timing of each table. 

  

In Commensal Hospitality, the diners enter, the door is shut, and the experience begins. It is much more like a theater performance in this way – everyone moves through the experience at the same pace and time. 

  

Beyond the obvious benefit of reducing complexity and labor, this has three added benefits

  

First, No Hidden Kitchen

  

Typically, the restaurant’s kitchen is hidden to the diner. This is so for a number of reasons, but principally it is because the kitchen is chaotic and loud, and the dining room is intended to be serene and orderly. The kitchen is understandably intense because the people working in it are, as aforementioned, involved in making dozens of dishes at the same time. 

  

In our version of Commensal Hospitality, there is an open kitchen – a fluid and singular space that holds both the dining room and kitchen. This design decision is better on a number of axes:

1. Does not alienate the guest from the source of their food. You see the people making the food and plating the food – you can draw a line between what you eat and who made it. It brings the chefs into the forefront, as humans, and makes possible a wider range of experiential possibilities between diner, ingredients, and chef. 
    
2. Makes it easier for chefs to be involved in the dining experience. Moreover, they can observe the dining room from the kitchen perch, which minimizes information asymmetry and latency. In a standard kitchen, chefs only know the status of the dining room through the servers, which leads to error and wasted time. 
    
3. It spatially communicates to guests that this is a home-like space, which helps to set the mood for communal eating.
    
4. One of the great joys of cooking is witnessing people eat your food. An open kitchen humanizes the experience for the chef. 
    

  

Second, A Human Experience

  

You could go to the same NYC restaurant a half dozen times, and have a different server every time. It is probably more likely than unlikely – restaurants must carry a large base of servers, given the expected transience and sub-40 hr/week work schedules. 

  

Restaurants live-and-die by their repeat customers, yet it seems most restaurants have little interest in developing the human relationships that make repeated patronage likely. We like going to the same Bodega, or coffee shop, not because the food or drink is better than the next place, but because the act of going recognizes our humanity. 

  

In Commensal Hospitality, a small team organizes and facilitates the experience every night. Diners meet the same team, every visit. This generates the important relationship between business and patron, and human and human, which also generates the comfort to return to a space where meeting strangers is the default. We must demonstrate the same sincerity and humanity which we wish diners to extend to one another. During each dinner, you not only meet the fellow diners, but everyone on the team making the restaurant possible. High-end restaurants will attempt to do this for their diners – bringing them into the kitchen, sending out the chef for small talk, and the like – but they are limited by a model of many-worlds hospitality. Here, that experience is a constant. 

  

We want to generate the feeling in each person that this is “their place”. Yes, we want to serve great food, but there is no monopoly to be had with great food alone – NYC has some of the best food anywhere. Yet, the feeling of connection, between the restaurant and the dinner and the diner and other diners – that is special and unique. 

  
  

Third, An Expanded Experience

  

With everyone dining at the same table, at the same time, we can spend several X the time speaking with and crafting an experience for our diners. The chefs can come out for each course and actually talk about the food and produce and producers, because they need not be in the kitchen preparing other table’s courses. The Experience Designer can then be a real facilitator, because they too are not managing other tables or tasks. 

  

This is an impossibility in a conventional restaurant. Servers and chefs are limited by their involvement in any one table because they are managing other tables and courses simultaneously. If you are an important guest, the chef may come out and talk for a minute. If they did that at every table, for every course, they would no longer be a chef. Moreover, having been a server myself, you have to move swiftly and on partial-autopilot. The server is keeping track of a half-dozen tasks at once, so they are never fully present with you at the table; think about when you speak to someone, but are occupied with other thoughts – you can functionally hold a conversation, but you aren’t having a real conversation. Moreover, servers repeat the content, over and over, which tires and depresses. 

  

Restaurants talk a big game about telling a story through food or connecting people, but they are dragged down by their model of hospitality and service. 

### A Forgiving Experience

When you are engaged with others at a table, you eat slower. And, in good conversation, you are less concerned with the timing of courses. When the restaurants decenters food and recenters community, it provides the kitchen with more grace period. The kitchen can move at a slightly slower pace while a community experience unfolds. 

### Reservations are Easier

In city-life, social interactions are manufactured and planned. Most of your hang-out time with friends happens because someone coordinated a day, time and place. Restaurants are reliant on diners to complete the task of coordination and book a reservation. And, restaurants are subject to [rising rates of cancellations and no-shows](https://ny.eater.com/2024/11/19/24300312/cancelled-reservations-at-restaurants-fees-nyc) from these plans falling through. 

  

In commensal dining, reservations are principally made by individuals. In fact, we specifically encourage it. You come to dine with new people, not with those you have pre-established relationships with. If you want to develop and explore new relational possibilities, it is better to do so as an individual, so you aren’t constrained by the pre-existent social scripts and norms between you and your friends. As such, it’s so much easier to book a reservation; you aren’t reliant on a plan coming together, you can just come!

  

We are already seeing a rise in the solo-diner, as mentioned earlier, and commensal dining provides the opportunity to come as an individual and be a part of a group.**

### **Increased Revenue**

**Bleedout**

Restaurants typically have mixed table-sizes, which may track to a 2-top, a 4-top, and so on. Let's say your seating capacity is 50 people across 15 tables. At a given time, you may only have 35 people across those 15 tables (For example, you may have 1 person at a 2-top, or 3 at a 4-top). We can call this **bleedout.** This is not a marginal inefficiency, it is an everyday occurrence and inevitability for most restaurants.

In commensal dining, there are only empty chairs if the restaurant is unpopular. Any restaurant that does well in NYC must be busy, at least on Friday and Saturday Nights, so let’s take that as a given. What matters is how efficient the restaurant is when it's busy.

With communal tables, a busy night has every chair taken up. Diners are a part of a shared experience, so no seat is left unfilled because it is at **someone else's table**. Conversely, a typical restaurant may be “full”, have a waitlist of 50 parties and still not be truly _**full.**_

**Turning Tables**

In a restaurant, the same table can account for multiple parties over a single service. That is, if a party sits down at 5:30, we could also book a 7:30 reservation at the same table (or seat a walk-in at that time). So a 4-top could potentially accommodate 8 people over a 4-hour service window. However, because the customer has the choice to choose exactly when they come in, often we don't get to turn that table more than once. Maybe the first seating at that 4-top is at 7:00, and you won't be able to accommodate a group of 4 who inquire about an 8:00pm sitting. If the two groups were at 6:00 and 8:00, that'd be great, but it didn't line up that way. So, that one 4-top that can sit 8 over the course of a night, now only sat 4. For that table, we're just operating at 50% efficiency.

With set seating times, at 5:30 and 8, the restaurant is full for five straight hours, from 5:30-10:30. Every seat gets used twice, once at each set time. In a normal restaurant, with the same dinner hours, they might sit a 6-top at 7:00. Set seating times ensures you get **two seatings out of every chair**.

Both of these restaurant dining room drawings have the same sq ft. One seats 12, and the other 18. In the first of these dining rooms, tables are spaced out from one another so that each party is insulated from all other diners in the space. Moreover, the server needs a walkway in between each table. In the second of these, diners eat together. Where there was previously a “walkway”, there is now table space, and customers sit comfortably next to one another, rather than at considerable distance.

This is a crude example, but it illustrates a simple and general truth: **restaurants with larger parties can sit more people**. At Garnet, all nighttime dining is done with others, whether in a group of 6 or a group of 12, allowing for a more efficient use of sq ft.

**Other Areas of Increased Revenue**

1. **Easier Reservations**
    1. In city-life, we spend time with friends because someone coordinated a day, time and place. Restaurants are reliant on diners to complete the task of coordination to book a reservation. And, restaurants are subject to [rising rates of cancellations and no-shows](https://ny.eater.com/2024/11/19/24300312/cancelled-reservations-at-restaurants-fees-nyc) from these plans falling through. **In commensal dining**, reservations are principally made by individuals. In fact, we specifically encourage it. As such, it’s so much easier to book a reservation; you aren’t reliant on a plan coming together, you can just come!
2. **Customer Loyalty and Retention**
    1. Garnet is designed for repeatability: guests sign up for or build habits around communal meals, returning weekly or monthly, making the LTV of each customer much higher than at most NYC restaurants
3. **A Meaningful Experience**
    1. At most restaurants, you pay for the food and preparation and service. Here, you are paying for something more important, allowing us to further decouple the price from the food.

### Lower Labor Costs

In the P&L breakdown of many restaurant, **labor can near 50%**. Depending on the kind of restaurant, the % will be different: yet, at almost all restaurants, labor is the single biggest cost by a wide margin. The single most important question for a restaurant’s profitability is **how can you bring labor costs down?**

At Garnet, **we have very few Front of House (FOH) roles**. There are no servers, no General Manager, and no host/hostess. There are only two roles – an Experience Designer and an operations assistant – who serve as facilitators and conductors for the guest experience. Since the whole dining room is sat at once, and treated as a whole, we don’t need servers as intermediaries between the kitchen and the dining room. Just like that, we’ve reduced our headcount by upwards, or more then, 50%.


Moreover, **we’ll have fewer chefs too.** How?

| **Efficiency Factor** | **Traditional**                                                | **Garnet**                                                           |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Menu Format**       | Lengthy a la carte menu requires more prep and flexibility     | Streamlined set-menu; no a la carte                                  |
| **Dietary Model**     | Omnivore menu = more substitutions and allergen considerations | Plant-based menu = fewer substitutions, less prep                    |
| **Service Style**     | Multiple tables and courses at once = more chefs needed        | Set seatings, single experience = no need to juggle multiple courses |
| **Order Management**  | Ticketing system requires expeditor to manage flow             | No ticketing system = no expeditor required                          |
| **Plating**           | Individual plating for each diner is more time-consuming       | Some family-style courses = less time plating individual dishes      |

### Lower COGS

At Garnet, we’ll also be able to reduce COGS. How will we accomplish this?

| **Cost & Margin Strategy**    | **Traditional**                                                       | **Garnet**                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Local & Seasonal Sourcing** | Out-of-season and distributor-sourced ingredients = higher cost       | Use seasonal produce, sourced directly from farmers = lower cost, fewer intermediaries |
| **Highlighting Plants**       | Animal proteins (meat, fish, etc…) are high-cost                      | No meat or fish = cost savings                                                         |
| **Low Waste**                 | A la carte = overordering + unpredictable waste                       | Set menus + bookings = accurate ordering; scraps repurposed into pantry staples        |
| **Community vs. Luxury**      | Price justified through expensive ingredients like truffles or caviar | Value-driven pricing informed by community, not luxury status symbols                  |

### **Simpler and Better Business**

> By operating a simpler business, we get to spend more time on the things that matter.

**Benefits of Reduced Labor**

> _As I whined about in the article, little of my day to day has anything to do with cooking. My life has become an unending chore of managing interpersonal conflicts, time off requests and scheduling concessions. – Eric Huang of Pecking House_

Restaurant employees – and specifically FOH employees – are hard to find, schedule, and retain. Most often, servers will serve as a means to make money for some more primary pursuit of theirs. Their job as a server is not the primary interest, and so building culture and a sense of pride in their work is difficult. They are transient, so as a GM you are consistently looking for and onboarding staff. As they have other pursuits, scheduling FOH is time-consuming and tricky. Not only is the labor traditionally the greatest cost, but it is also the greatest time sync - interviewing, hiring, onboarding, managing, scheduling, payroll, and more.

With less labor, **we’ll free up a ton of time to work on the things that matter**. Too many restaurant owners are weighed down by the minutiae of a large team, so they direct all their time to staying afloat rather than the compounding effects of progressively improving the operation and experience.

**Higher Salaries, More Creativity, Better Retention**

In most restaurants, servers have the highest annual income of any employee. Chefs, dishwashers, and others, meanwhile, have annual incomes that can be hard to live by. Since we have no servers, the “tip”, which will be in the form of a transparent 20% service charge, will be distributed across the whole team. As we have a small team, we expect that **each employee will make a total salary that is higher than most other restaurants in NYC**.

Each chef will also have more creativity and more customer interaction that at a conventional restaurant. Unlike working in a windowless back-room, chefs will have view into a dining room of people enjoying their food. As our team is small, it won’t be structured like a typical brigade system (i.e. garde manger), so chefs will be able to exercise creativity in diverse ways.

With higher salaries, and more nourishing work, we anticipate that we’ll can **attract, hire, and retain the best talent**. Ultimately, the success of any business comes down to the talent density of the people who make it up.

**Payments outside the Restaurants**

When you get rid of servers and the experience of paying via a check, you instead take money ahead of time. Why does this help?

1. **Money, earlier.** The restaurant has money in the bank before, rather than after, the food is served. So, the business can access capital days or weeks before the typical restaurant. Accessing capital earlier simply makes everything easier, from purchasing products to paying staff to investing in business-improvements.
2. **Easier and More Accurate Accounting.** Accounting is easier when there is a flat price that everyone pays, and that money all comes in online. No inputting checks and tips at the end of the night, which results in human error.
3. **Easier and Earlier P&L Projections.** When the price is standardized, and the restaurant is not subject to the personalized orders of its diners, we can more accurately project revenue figures. If we need to raise prices, introduce more offerings, or make other business decisions, we’ll know well before the typical restaurant would.
4. **Purer Experience.** Money depresses. Why should you have to end a great experience with the exchange of money?
