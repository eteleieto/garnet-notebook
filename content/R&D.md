
Over the past year, we've been doing a lot of R&D for the restaurant, using regional ingredients, upcycled byproducts, and both traditional and novel forms of fermentation. 

We're growing koji and tempeh on local grains, preserving dozens of different grown and foraged items, and doing curiosity-driven flavor research in an effort to develop a possible language for a distinctly Northeastern United States food identity.

Why does any of this matter? Taste, belonging and ethics...

**Taste**

Restaurants should pursue deliciousness. 

Great food cultures are born not from abundance, but from regional constraints. Garum, the fermented fish sauce of the Roman Empire, transformed fish guts into a shelf-stable source of umami. In the Alps, milk couldn’t travel down into the valleys before it spoiled, so it was fermented into hard, long-aged cheeses like Comté and Gruyère. 

We have long winters here, so fermentation and preservation extend the season and make use of kitchen-trim.

Great dishes that define cuisines come from sourcing well and complex fermented flavors:  
think of the best raw fish with soy sauce and vinegared rice or a perfectly ripe tomato with aged balsamic. Source the best produce, in peak season, couple it with something fermented, and treat it with care, technique and love. 


**Belonging**

This is a restaurant that principally cares about generating belonging. Part of that is through human relations, both strong and weak ties, through the age old civic tradition of eating with your neighbors

Yet, another part, under-discussed amongst "community" minded people, is one's relation to place and region. 

I believe eating is one of our best available entrances into this relation. 

We know this all intuitively when we travel. You go somewhere, and you learn about it through food. You go into a stranger's home, and they welcome you with their region's food.  

it the language of the place, a fundamental unit of culture. Regional food identity is majorly important to belonging. 

In NYC, our urban eating experience feels very abstracted from place. Most of the food we eat, shaped by shelf stability and scale, is trucked from thousands of miles away, nutritionally hollow, flavorless, and aggressively processed. I think this leads to profound spiritual alienation. Our foods are totally unspecific, a food system of nowhere in particular. As such, we have a profound narrowing of culinary, botanical, and cultural diversity. 

Part of the goal of the restaurant is to develop our regional food culture, for taste and belonging.

The road away from alienation is through coming to know the food of this place, with the people with whom you live near. 

And, the road to a richer ecology of place, starts here too. The urbanite is embedded in a system of necessary relations with the ruralite. What we eat in the city directly shapes the non-urban environment. Our collective choices determine how soil is treated, what kinds of farms are viable, and what our landscapes become.

Opening up a restaurant that experiments with the development of community. Over dinner, people will come in as individuals, or in small groups, and engage in this age old civic tradition of eating with others. the restaurant, lightly facilitating that experience. 

In a typical restaurant, especially the more elevated and high end you get, the dining experience is like a lecture or theatre. You are the audience, there to receive and accept. 

---

When you are sitting with neighbors and strangers, people you don't know just yet, you have the opportunity to recreate the experience as a constructive activity. That is, you and those around you, like a seminar class, investigate your experience. Everyone turns their attention on the shared intimate experience of imbibing, of having a glass of wine. Unlike a painting, you are literally ingesting this thing, opening your body, in front of others, going from the abstract speak of aesethics to a lived and felt version that you build up from scratch.  

---

In this case, don't know the wine. So have to really dial attention, and thus experience becomes a practice of paying attention. And we see how paying attention is really the act of developing relations, between you and the drink, you and the craftsperson behind it, the drink and its possible origin. 

Art is not a class of objects, but a way of experiencing. Art, then, is not separate from life; it’s what happens when life is lived with full attention and participation. not displayed art, but enacted art. 

We want you all to see that, together, we co-constitute a shared world, with a shared vocabulary. 

---
As you do so, we're also begetting relations with others around us, because we're constructing a world with them. And through sharpening our attention, we can also attend to others more richly. 

That’s profoundly social. It’s how community starts, not through ideology or belief, but through shared perception. 

---

But I also think there is more here. Can phenomenology beget ethics?

Because ethics, in its earliest form, is _attunement_,  being responsive to others.  
And phenomenology, done together, trains that capacity. It’s a way of learning to be _with._ Empathy with co-attention

So this wine tasting isn’t about mastery or correctness. It’s an exercise in _intersubjectivity_... how individual perception becomes communal meaning.
