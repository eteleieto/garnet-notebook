Over the past ten years, I’ve lived in and organized communes, ecovillages, alternative educational centers and co-living spaces, studying how coherence, belonging, and trust form. In every place, the same pattern: those who ate together, lived well together. The best communities, with the strongest relationships, the greatest cohesion and collaboration, ate together, regularly. As I designed my own community spaces, this was the most reliable structure. 

Shared meals didn’t just sustain the group, they were also how strangers became part of it.

- People go back to restaurants to be cared for. not food

The owners and staff of spaces learn so much about their customers. They see the papers they read, the attitudes they hold, and glimpse into their interests and curiosities from conversations. These are rarely tracked and documented, but the spaces we, humans, tend to enjoy and patronize, over and over, have employees who remember these small details. 

  

It is the remembering of a person, the acknowledgment of that memory, and the expressed contentment in seeing that person again that creates feelings of warmth and intimacy. It is these feelings which people want, and which inform their movements through a city of unending storefronts. It is a mistake to think that these feelings are principally generated through interior design decisions. Those decisions surely matter, too, and inform one’s feelings and movements, but it is authentic human interaction which conveys the feelings of intimacy and warmth in a storefront space. 

  

Think about sitting with a group of friends on the floor of an unfurnished apartment, backs hunched over takeout containers and plastic utensils. These feelings are transmitted between people mostly, and secondarily between people and space (or people and X, Y, Z). 

  

At the cafe I mentioned earlier, Nako, the space had the coldness of an AI-generated coffee shop. Yet, the owner, Erik, took the moment, before asking what I would like to order, to ask how I was. I answered, and asked him too. Then, he asked what I’d like to order. The micro-interaction unfolded at the pace of a human interaction, not a commoditized one. The space was, for my tastes and in isolation, close to awful, but I sat there for several hours and felt it to be intimate and warm. 

  

It is for this reason that you have platforms, like that which Maggie Tang is working on ([Loyalist](https://www.loyalist.com/)), dedicated to assisting restaurants in this work. 

  

So, part of this is: Can the storefront have an excellent memory? Can a more casual spot have the attentiveness, without the pretension, of a place like Eleven Madison Park? I very much think so.

Why would you expect the project of a mid 20s, without an exhaustive and pedigreed restaurant resume, to open up something great in dining? I don’t wish to rely on the stories of others to convince you of my own capacity, but maybe a few references will assuage those with concerns. 

  

My recent friend Ethan wrote a piece last year, called “The Other 27 Club”, publichse in the Art of Eating Magazine It is an excellent piece, and a worthwhile read. In it, Ethan draws out a thread – the great American restaurants of the last century have been opened up by graduates of the liberal arts with limited restaurant backgrounds. 

  

Alice Water studied French Cultural Studies at Berkeley. Inspired by her time abroad in France, she returned to California and opened Chez Panisse at 27. 

  

Danny Meyers studied political science at Trinity College, traveled to France and Italy, and opened up Union Square Cafe at age 27. 

  

Dan Barber studied political science and English at Tufts, and after moving to LA to attempt life as a Novelist, figured he’d rather be a chef. He worked for a few restaurants (including Chez Panisse), and then opened Dan Barber Catering, which would then become Blue Hill, at 27.  

  

David Chang also had a liberal arts background, before opening Momofuku Noodle Bar at 27. The same is true of Eddie Huang and Baohaus.  

  

Blake then writes

  

I’m not saying you have to study college-level humanities to open a groundbreaking

American restaurant. Thomas Keller, for example, had a purely culinary education and

opened the French Laundry at 39. Rather, there is a consistent type of chef whose tastes, gumption, social concern, love for the arts and quality of life, together with a gastronomic affinity and (in all fairness) family financial support, make them more likely to create a highly original new restaurant. These five aren’t “chefs” so much as business-savvy epicureans whose urges happened to express themselves in restaurants.

  

I didn’t myself finish college, though I was, and am, a liberal arts kid. Moreover, I don’t have any family financial support. And, even more, I’m weary of providing these reference points as some suggestion that I may be alike these individuals. ### Garnet as particpatory research model

If the restaurant is a research organism, then its participants are not subjects but collaborators. Knowledge is co-created through reciprocal awareness: the staff’s attentiveness to guests, guests’ attentiveness to one another, and the restaurant’s attentiveness to itself.

The role of the restaurant becomes one of facilitation rather than control — designing conditions that allow consciousness to emerge. Spatial cues, rituals, and pacing all serve as invitations to awareness. The service itself becomes an experiment in distributed intelligence: how does a group of strangers collectively produce comfort, curiosity, and connection?

This shifts the ethic of hospitality. It is no longer about perfection of service, but about cultivation of understanding — the shared inquiry into how we inhabit space and one another’s company.
