The internet makes it easy to meet people who are like you. 

Like ceramics? You can follow other ceramicists, get suggested ceramics events, and with relative ease meet others offline. 

What seems more difficult is creating relationships among those who live nearby. 

Related: 
1. [[Neighborly Relations]]. There are many kinds of relationships [[The Taxonomy of Relationships]], and I think we don't talk enough about our neighborly ones.

Basic normative claim: heterogeneity is good. 

geographical proximity matters for few reasons
1. proximity is everything is relationships. the not-structured time matters a lot, ONCE you have had single structure. you don't want [[a string of overdue coffee chats]]. [[Relationships are plagued by their beginnings]]
	1. [[structuring the threshold]] and recurring encounters, ritual. 
2. Proximate relationships draw you out into a shared physical world. A problem on the street: "oh that's Jerry's house". You see the newly planted flowers, and you thank Greg for it. You bump in and run in. It creates shared concern, stewardship, etc..
	1. [[The number of neighbors you know is the most important indicator of civic health]]

Structure matters at the point of possible interaction. after that, proximity does a lot of the work. Instead of a curated community, which constantly hosts events and gatherings, just get people to see each other often. It's the same as the coffee chat. You become reliant on a coordinating event, rather than a happenstance interaction. 

Cities already have people in incredibly close proximity. The problem is getting them interacting. 

An eighth floor office building has less relation to place then the ground-level unit there. The storefront is at the street. Passerbys peer in, happenings on the street are right outside the window. The boundaries between street and it aren't clear. 

Some places end when you leave. An office is sort of like this. A great ground-level place is different. 

- **Entry porosity:** How much selection is required before I can come inside?
- **Social porosity:** How likely am I to interact with people I don't already know?
- **Difference porosity:** How much heterogeneity can the space accommodate, rather than selecting for similarity?
- **Boundary porosity:** How much do relationships generated inside spill outward into the surrounding street/neighborhood?

I guess the basic claim is this: we over-invest assembling the right kind of people, and under-invest in helping proximate people be meaningful to one another. 

This doesn't mean you should choose your friends. On the contrary, I think [[friendship is the greatest worldly good]] and perhaps the single most important thing to get right. 

Storefronts interesting because they take an indiscriminate public realm and the structured private realm, and place them immediately next to each other.
