
[[Non Alcoholic Beverages]]

#### General Ethos
We believe that to feel connected to a place, and to take care of it, requires knowing it. It requires knowing the people in your neighborhood, the changes in the seasons, and the land that surrounds you.

Garnet is an ecologically and relationally driven restaurant, at several scales: in the restaurant, on the block, in the neighborhood, in the city, and across the region. Region and community are not separate concerns. To explore the region is to explore the place in which we live; spatial, geographic, and relational knowledge are central to belonging. To know a place is to feel a part of it, and to feel a part of it is to care for it. 

At its core, our beverage program is about cultivating a sense of belonging. We approach that work through two complementary lenses: regionalism and communalism.

The beverage program operates within the same bounds, and with the same ethos, as the food program. This seems like an obvious extension, but while localism has influenced food menus in the last several decades, it has barely touched beverage menus.

The food we serve is alive. We serve radishes which were picked yesterday alongside fermented radish, signed by the microbial signature of its origin. Our dishes are of a time and a place; the beverages should be too.

We serve a rotating regional beverage program, with a focus on minimal intervention beverages. In the Northeast US, there is a rich history of cider-making, and so cider will be a focal point of our program. In winemaking, it is easier to grow hybrids with minimal intervention than vinifera, and so we’ll feature them heavily. Our choices are emergent out of the conditions of the region.

The first concern is regional. Beverages are doorways into understanding a place. They put diners into contact with the people, landscapes, seasons, and conditions that make up our region. Through close relationships with producers, we come to know the region in which we reside; through the beverage program, we share that knowledge with our guests.

We heavily prize relationships. We care to know our producers well, and to work with producers who take care of their land and their people. In doing so, our beverage program is reflective of dialogues with our extended community.

For example, we recently took a trip to the Finger Lakes and Hudson Valley, where we met with producers like Apollo’s Praise, Red Tail Ridge, Usonia, Osmote, Eminence Road, Behemoth, Ria’s, and others.

We’re uniquely excited to highlight small producers, like John Albert or Matt at Entelecheia, who make exceptional small-batch, opinionated wines that demonstrate a rich understanding of their home. Neither John nor Matt have their wines on a NYC restaurant list, and we’re thrilled to share their work and accumulated knowledge with the city.

The second interest is communal. We are curious about the pro-social dimensions of beverages and how they shape attention, generosity, and relation at the table. A drink gives us something to hold, to sip, to do — something to keep our hands engaged while we listen or speak. When you pour tea for a stranger, what happens between those two people? When you hold a warm cup of tea in both hands, or a bubbling, high-acid kombucha, does your orientation toward another change? When you find a special 750ml bottle of cider at the bar, and bring it back to the table to share, how does that information asymmetry — between the purchaser, who learned about the cider, and the other diners, who only know what is shared with them — change the quality of the gift?

We favor drinks and methods that are tools for conviviality.

Because our beverage program diverges strongly from the conventional Euro-centric lists of NYC, guests will often have lower-than-average context. We take on the productive burden of guiding and informing. This is not incidental to the program.

Our model of service, wherein we speak to the whole room at once, also gives us the opportunity to create shared context. Whereas a server in a traditional service format is juggling multiple independent tables at once, and can never spend much time with any one table, here we can tell the room what they are drinking, where it comes from, who made it, and why it matters.

So, our beverage program operates in three related ways:

1. To know, ourselves, the region in which we reside, through close relationships with producers.
2. To share a range of beverages that introduce guests to the many places, people, and conditions that make up our region.
3. To consider how the qualities of a beverage – what it is, how it is shared, and with whom – support the development of human relations at the table.

Both regionalism and communalism are in service of belonging.

#### Program Overview
The evening beverage program is broken down across three categories: pairings, BTG and BTB. 

As we work with many small producers – and as the menu changes often - the beverage list will fluctuate regularly. 

Our BTB alcoholic offerings are organized by region and producer. 

The "list" is a map of the territory, with producers as landmarks. Each region will begin with producer highlights, which share the story of their practice and our relationship. By centering place and person, we unsettle the conventional beverage taxonomy. Wines, ciders, co-ferments, meads, beers, and the like are scattered across the map, with no beverage-type taking precedence over the other. Some regions will of course have disproportionate numbers of certain beverages, so the diner will learn that the finger lakes is a wine region that makes world-class rieslings and that Hudson Valley is made up of a rich history of uncultivated apple ciders. 

We'll also offer greater variation in bottle size than a typical list, with inclusions of 375ml and magnums, in addition to the typical 750ml sizing. 

For pairings, we'll offer a 3-pour pairing, available in both an alcoholic and non-alcoholic option. The pairings will sit around $45 and $35 respectively, and like the rest of the program, will include, and go beyond, wine. The pairing is substantial enough to accompany a diner through their meal, while leaving ample room for further discovery over the course of the evening. 

We likely won't offer cocktails to start, but we will have a small selection of after dinner drinks, like Wild Arcs' Black Walnut Nocino, a local pommeau, or an in-house digestif. 

---
Dinners also begin with a welcome drink

The welcome drink will change with the seasons, but often be a warm or iced roasted grain tea. Grains are an essential feature of our menu, as they are an essential crop for the farmers we work with. Starting the dinner with a beverage made of a steeped whole grain is also a presentation of its essential flavor. This tea will be followed up by our bread course, a wildly different form of the same ingredients: water and grain.  

The welcome drink will be brought, in a teapot or pitcher, to each table as that table fills up. We'll prompt diners to pour for the person who sits down after them. This serves as the first gesture and suggested point of contact for two strangers sitting at the table. 

---
During the day, our beverage program will focus on affordability and offer a small selection of rotating drinks that pair well with our daytime offerings. 

We'll aim to always offer a drink that is a few dollars, or a lunch deal with a $2-3 drink add-on

---
Finally, the beverage program gives us a cadence for bringing producers directly into the life of the restaurant: special dinners where makers pour their beverages and join us at the table. The restaurant becomes a place for us to continue the dialogue with them. 

#### Non-Alc
The restaurant has a foundational belief that you shouldn't need alcohol to be in a room of strangers. It is our failing, as hosts, if diners default to alcohol for social comfort. We should aim to cater to a diner's tastes, values, and curiosities, wherever that may be an alcoholic or non-alcoholic drink. 

Our non-alcoholic beverage program will be diverse, intentioned, and as engaging as those beverages we purchase elsewhere. 

Our offerings will include foraged teas, grain and herbal infusions, wild-fermented sodas, kombuchas, amazakes, kefirs, ginger bugs, juices, malted milks, and otherwise. 

During the day, we'll explore familiar beverages made with regional ingredients. For example, a variant on root beer made from wild carbonated foraged beech leaves and sorghum molasses. 

We'll also bottle and cap some of our non-alcoholic beverages, so that diners may grab a 750ml at the bar and experience the comparable sensation of opening a bottle. 

#### The Bar
We have a bar, which stretches about 3/4 of the width of the room. The bar is standing only. 

At the beginning of the meal, guests will have a choice of a pairing – alc or non-alc – and a small BTG list to choose from. This choice is made at the table, on small cards that are tucked into the menu. Over the course of the meal, we don't take beverage orders at the table. If a diner forgoes this initial choice, they must come to the bar to order. When they are welcomed to the space, we'll encourage them to come to the bar to order drinks, meet other diners, and peer into the workings of the kitchen. 

The bar serves as a second social space, distinct from the table. It encourages movement through and within the dining room, turning the dining room into a fully navigable space (see [[Navigation is a defining feature of the domestic]]). For any reason, a guest may get up from the table and have a space to move into; thus, the bar serves as an escape hatch from stale conversation (see [[nothing lasts forever]]). 

The role of the bar has other compelling modalities, which we're keen to explore and document.

notes about supporting ny state wineries

High level: localism for food developed through chefs, farmer's market, ingredients, etc.. . Beverage culture develop through somms, importers, prestige regions, global comparison, etc.. . These are independent cultural developments.

I think the simplest answer is that farm-to-table changed kitchens more than it changed beverage programs.

1. Farm to table movement
    1. This mostly reached chefs and kitchens. It changed how they thought about sourcing. Chefs were going to the greenmarket, getting in produce and seeing an obvious quality difference that comes from caring about proximity and seasonality. Even though farm-to-table had ideological roots, it proliferated because local tastes better. it was a an ethic of deliciousness, not a land ethic. 
        1. Chefs get ingredients in every day. They can see obvious quality difference, as a function of local farms and shorter delivery times. farm to table was a call to not just a land ethic, but a quality and deliciousness ethic. And it was right.
    2. It didn't reach bev people, partly because drinks are fundamentally different. Drinks are aged and bottled. The time between a tomato picked and eaten matters, and gives the upstate tomato a major advantage it just tastes better. A bottle of wine can come from anywhere and not suffer a loss in quality, so the beverage person, optimizing for quality, chooses from the globe.

Important note

A restaurant can buy from Norwich Meadows, Eckerton, Lani’s, etc..., and still not be regional in any way. The produce is local, but the cuisine may be Italian, French, Japanese, whatever. The ingredients may be partly regional, but the restaurant’s view is still global.

That's why local beverages don’t, by default, fit. These wine, and other drinks, need a **cultural home**. They need a cuisine, a story, a real home. NYC has plenty of these amorphous market-driven restaurants (many very good!), but almost no restaurants asking with NY state cuisine is, what northeastern cuisine is, or the like.

The farm-to-table movement in the city changed produce purchasing, but it had little effect identity. It did not create a coherent regional cuisine. Without a cuisine, there is no obvious reason for the beverage program to be regional.

2. Self selection
    1. the people who built farms to table were chefs and farmers. the people who built restaurant wine culture were somms, importers, and FoH folks. Wine is about cosmopolitanism and sophistication and comparison. You flex by sharing knowledge across the globe, so ny state wines feel like concession.
3. The state of NY
    1. NYC also has a weird relationship to the state of NY. Boston feels obviously connected to MA, same as Portland with Maine and so one. But NYC is it's own place in many respects. It's an order of magnitude more cosmopolitan, transient and sprawl-y than any other Northeastern City. I think people in Portland feel an affinity to Maine. People in NYC feel affinity to NYC, not NY state. Affiliation doesn't extend in the same way it does for most cities. NYC doesn't take itself to be the city of NY, it's the city of the US or the world. In short, NYC has a sort of urban-localism, not a regional localism. NYers care about neighborhoods, blocks, boroughs... more than the state.
        1. Finger lakes is 5 hours away. that is genuinely far.
    2. Pride for place. In europe, in Tuscany, they really care for Tuscan wines. One, its a much bigger part of region historically. Two, italians have more patriotism to theur region than nyc folks for state or northeast. From hilltown to hilltown, people know the differences in food and wine. there isn't that granular understanding here for most people. regional food and bevc really defines their sense of belonging. it doesnt here. its, unfortunately, not a major part nyc experience and identity. id love to change that. it will help atomization and belonging and meaning problem. but this is reality. the food of NY is not connected to the ingredients here... hot dog, pretzel, bagel. it is connected to our global roots. unlike in italy.
4. Interface
    1. For ingredients, the greenmarket, and farmer's markets generally, created this recurring presence of regional agriculture within the city. You could understand crops and seasonal patterns. The cadence of markets allowed you to be a farm-to-table person at home, which also changed priorities and familiarities when you go to restaurant. Maybe you started to care more, or you knew what a sunchoke was, or realized that if you saw a local tomato it meant a better tomato. There is nothing comparable for beverages.
    2. NY restaurants have done very little to introduce NY wines. Serious wine lists may have one FLX riesling, as a gesture. If great restaurants don't serve it, guests won't learn about it.
5. On the Distributor Side
    1. The distributors carry a single regional wine, Zev maybe as the outlier here. So, if you want to carry NY wines, you have to work with tons and tons of distributors. And, if you want to be purely regional, you'll confront many distributors, each with their own case minimums, but only producer to fill those minimums out. It's way less messy to get multiple international wines through an established book. In produce, you can get a ton of local produce through Baldor, Lancaster, Myers, Hudson Harvest, Regional Access, etc.. imagine the mess it'd be if each distributor carried only a single local farmer... it wouldn't work. So, if you are sympathetic to, or even excited about NY wines, the system doesn't make it easy. It is easier to get wines from abroad than in-state. If it's easier, that's what people will do.
6. Comfort
    1. I think guests are willing to entertain more experimental food than wine. Wine, and beverage generally, is often a comfort zone for people. People opt for familiar grapes and regions, and markers of status. I think a lot of the best NY wines are not the wines trying to be something else, but ones that are truly reflective of the region. Sometimes, that means that they are less legible.
7. On the producer-side
    1. When we've gone up to FLX, we were surprised by how many makers had kind of given up on NYC (not a universal, but many anecdotal accounts). Many have an easier time selling to visitors in the tasting room – which, amongst other things, means sweeter wines, which are less for NYC – and selling to other cities like Boston, Philly, Chicago and DC. So, some producers veer away from NY tastes. And NYC folks who go to taste in FLX, in tasting at the big tasting rooms, might develop the view that the area doesn't have wine that suits the tastes they developed in the city. So there is feedback loop here. Obviously, I think there are exceptional producers in the region, In FLX, in Hudson Valley, in Long Island and elsewhere in the Northeast broadly. But, people have developed a view of the FLC that is hard to escape, even if there is a tranche of exceptional makers.
    2. I'd extend this beyond wine too, into co-ferments, ciders, meads, etc... NY has a long history of cider-making, and there are excellent cider producers. But NYC consumers think of cider as sweet. So a lot of the excellent makers aren't as much here
8. NYC history and today
    1. NY restaurants just is not a very regional place, historically. Refinement in NYC, going back to Delmonicos, was about grand service, being seen, imported luxury, and the like. Think about the staple food items here: pretzels, bagels, pizza, hot dogs. NY's restaurant identity has been global: Italian, French, Jewish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Caribbean, Mexican, Indian, etc... This is large part of what makes NYC incredible. But it also means that New York has not developed the same kind of regional restaurant culture that exists elsewhere. There are contemporary American restaurants here, but there aren' really many regional restaurants in the way there are in California or Louisiana or many other states. They are American is a kind of ambiguous, market and chef-driven way (e.g. Four Horseman). They go the farmer's market and they use local produce because it tastes excellent, but they aren't trying to express a coherent cuisine, so they aren't bound or swayed by regional wines. Just look at the Michelin list. We have something like 70 starred restaurants in NY. Blue Hill is, maybe, the only regional restaurant on the list. If you go almost anywhere else globally, the list is chock full of regional establishments. The NYC list is becoming less French heavy, and there are now as many or more Korean or Japanese places as French, so you get a shift that includes sake, soju and more which feels now more accepted. Take Lei, championing chinese wines. NYC has moved, culinarly, beyond europe, but not looked yet at itself. Our bev is moving away from french/italian canon, but it is still far from taking on FLX wines, hudson valley ciders, and the exciting murky territory of other "wines" and coferments. What I see here, in part, is that NYC is anti-provincial (again, not anti-local, since NYers care a lot about a certain scale of localism). The restaurants, the fashion, the music, the art... it is all in global conversation. NY state wine maybe feels too provincial for that reason. The NYC person isn't provincial, or else maybe they wouldn't be here.

Answer to second question: this can change, if a great restaurant/s, bars, shops champion them as truly fantastic beverages rather than some farm-to-table compromise. If a few, or even one, of these places make the comittment and deal with the challeneg, a lot will change. the producers will turn their attention to nyc, the great rpodcuers will get more support, and new producers will sprout up. the disibutros may pick up ore, and make it easier. others will adapt it. i think for it to happen, it wont just be wine. NY's best producers will span categories. i think cider and the longtail of other beverages will be part of the mix. I also think it won't be simply vinifera, but lots of hybrids of course too. I dont think itll take much for it to change.

from [[Adam Moritz]]

Lots of good stuff, and much overlap with some of the places where I was thinking. I think your point about the difference between regionalism in ingredients not equating to regionalism in cuisine is one of the key ones here.

  

Another important point (which dovetails with your answer to the second q) is that wine (and winemaking specifically) moves quite slowly, really on the individual producer level. It often takes a number of vintages for winemakers to come into mastery over what they're doing—many of the great "natural" winemakers in France are longstanding domains which are able to a) take more risks (as any one vintage matters less when you are more established) and b) are able to begin with a great deal more understanding about their region/grape varieties. Even the most iconoclastic winemaker of the 90s began often from winemaking families. We don't have that same tradition in New York.

  

So, especially among winemakers which are working within a style more in line with what young people are drinking (lower extraction, better, more "natural" farming, maceration), there is a lot of learning that is happening. And since many of the winemakers we met working with this ethos started in the last 3-5 years (COVID), there is just a learning curve. There are many early successes, but I think FLX wine is going to get really really interesting in the next 3-5 years, as these winemakers (Usonia, Behemoth, John Albert, Ria's etc. etc.) come into their own, both in their winemaking but also (and this moves slower than winemaking expertise) in their farming. Barbichette got a headstart because they buy their fruit from essentially the best grower in the FLX. But this is a reciprocal process, they need to be interacting with people drinking their wines, and need to know that those people are discerning and understand what they're trying to do. This encourages them to continue, especially when the community in the FLX is often of a very different mind when it comes to levels of intervention in the vineyard and the cellar.

  

I think that recieved styles are generally _not_ as important as winemakers think—New York diners and drinkers care less about specific varietals and tried and true styles than any other in history, it's much more virtues based, ideological, and narrative. A winemaker with a good story and someone in the city to tell it will sell much better than a sweet riesling. Honestly the preconceptions of those style (riesling, gewurztraminer) are actually more hindering than they are helpful. So, Ciders, Non-vinifera, etc. But it needs to be delicious and serious. Or at very least have a seriousness spectrum. I assume as many winemakers age into their practice the wines will get more serious.  

  

Essentially, all of this relies _both_ on the winemakers and the institutions that serve their wines. We are lucky here that we can have a closer feedback loop than we can have with Old world winemakers (who have much more longstanding traditions that align/have shaped the current tastes in NY).
