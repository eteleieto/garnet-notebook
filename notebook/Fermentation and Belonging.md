Yet, here we're talking about regional food, where does fermentation come into play?

Fermented foods are the keystone items of most cuisines. 

They are distinctive markets of cuisine – kimchi in Korea, stinky cheeses in France, half sours in NYC, and so on. And as you get more local, you get more distinctive varieties. 

They are punchy, funky, concentrated, delicious, and recognizable flavors, so they become recognizable features of a cuisine. As these cuisines developed, in a time before refrigeration and globalized food supply, communities needed methods to extend the growing seasons and develop shelf stable products. Fermentation was a common preservation device. Preserved, these creations were available year round, which gave them chance to be incorporated in dishes at any time, and thus to become staples of the cuisine. 

So, when we speak of creating, and connecting to, a cuisine of the Northeast US, it begets us to speak of, and to make use of, fermentation.

#### Person and Place

Fermentation always bears the mark of the person and place from which it comes. Each of us carries our own microbial signature. So too do regions, plants, and soil. A simple lacto-ferment, done with the same technique, will be different here than there. Ferments are literally alive out of, and through, us and our environment. To make a ferment is a collaborative ecological act. The person and the place is embedded in the form, which makes sharing and consuming it a intimate relational act – you are literally consuming the unique signature of person and place. 


#### Labor and Savor

Historically, across the Northeast US, and in much of the world, ferment-ing was a way of extending the abundance of the growing season through the colder months. 

That abundance, and the processing required to store it, was done collectively. 

Take sauerkraut. Families and neighbors would gather to chop, salt, and jar many, many cabbages. This salty and lactic-y cabbage fed whole families and neighborhoods, especially before the advent of refrigeration. People fermented in bulk. The act of fermenting was a communal act. When the sauerkraut was ready, it was often more than any household could consume, so it was shared. Fermentation was a solution to scale. It created, and creates, moments for communal processing and monitoring, gift and reception, and seasonal rituals and traditions that function as central community structures. 

Fermentation was a method of collective resilience through collective making. 

Fermenting, today, is not fully consistent with its collectivist origins, but it has carried forth some similar features. 

When we work with farmers, they will often have too much of an item. We can take those bulk items and ferment them to extend their use over time. We cannot serve 50 lbs of blueberry before they rot, but we can ferment them for the winter. This possibility strengthens the relationship between the chef and the farmer, and attunes us to the calendrical rhythms of our shared region. 

#### Trust 

[[Joshua Evans]] describes fermentation as "culturally appropriate, socially significant, carefully controlled rot."

That it rots in the right way requires trust in the person or people who made it. 

Ferments are alive with microbes, of unknown and profound variability. You may look at a lacto-ferment, and see it literally bubbling. 

To eat another's ferment, then is to place trust in their care and knowledge, and in doing so, to forge a social bond with them. 

It's the culinary equivalent of the idiomatic trust fall. 

#### Temporality

Time is an essential variable in the experience of culinary care. An Italian-American restaurant offers their Sunday sauce, simmering since the morning. A ramen restaurant, or a pho shop, too offers their broth that cooks for "8-hours". Pujol's mole madre, the central component in their canonical dish, has aged for 2500+ days. When you go to a friend's home, and they have a pot of anything that has been tended to for hours, we feel uniquely cared for. 

Time is care. When place advertises time, they are advertising care (and flavor, too). 

Ferments require days, months, or years to mature. And, time is a pre-condition. That is, the Sunday sauce leverages time as a indicator of quality, but sauce does not require time. Ferments require time. Miso needs, at minimum, weeks, and more often months. It is not shortcutable. 

Thus, when we we share ferments, and the time that went into their creation, we are demonstrating our interest in you and into developing a relationship.

#### Ferments and Conversation

Social acts are embedded in the practice of making and sharing ferments. 

At communal tables, amongst mostly strangers, we're trying to bring people out into social space. 

There are many such ways to do this, but one method is to introduce new flavors. 

Have you ... "what is that?", "I've never had that flavor before", "it's kind of like this or like this..."

When we try something new, taste an unknown flavor, we turn to others to figure what that flavor is. Each person, having ingested the same thing, seeks out the other. The aim is not to explicitly challenge one's preferences, but to introduce a bit of unfamiliar space in the territory. Confronted with the unknown and the ill-defined, conversation is the exploratory tool. 


At the restaurant, as we develop [[Regional Food Culture]], ferments will be our frequented partner in developing these novel ingredients. 

Thus, ferments are the stimulus, that bubble into conversation.

#### Waste

Restaurants generate immense amount of waste, from vegetable trim to stale bread to bruised fruit. Through fermentation, we can take these impending waste streams and upcycle them into fantastic and desirable flavors. 

Fundamentally, low-waste cooking is communally-oriented cooking. It shows respect to the ingredient, and the many contributors who enabled its growth. It a tacit acknowledgment of our unimaginably excessive food waste. By understanding and utilizing the whole ingredient, it is, again, a demonstration of understanding and care. ==An attempt at a unity of the ingredient mirrors the wholeness and integration of the person. ==
