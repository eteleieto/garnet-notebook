Space as Intellectual Practice

  

The space is self-adapting, because the method of study (community) is also the object of study (community). We study community, by being in community, and through being observant of the community then reform structures and ideas for future settings. 

  

Each of these settings is exploring the possiblity of form - spatial form, community form, norms, etc.. and the diners are involved in that collective undertaking of understanding what these are, and whether they work, and why…

Documentation Methods:

1. Participatory Research Framework
    

- Create simple research questions for each dinner series (e.g., "How do repeated meals affect feelings of neighborhood belonging?")
    
- Design lightweight data collection that feels natural within the dining experience
    
- Train staff as participant-observers who can capture insights without disrupting experiences
    

3. Customer-Led Documentation
    

- Provide beautiful journals at each dinner where participants can record observations
    
- Create structured reflection prompts between courses
    
- Design simple before/after surveys that feel conversational rather than clinical
    
- Create a "field notes" section in your digital app where members can contribute observations
    

5. Physical Documentation Center
    

- Dedicate a visible corner of the space as the "Community Research Hub"
    
- Display emerging findings in accessible, visually engaging ways
    
- Invite customers to contribute their own observations and hypotheses
    
- Host quarterly "research review" events where findings are shared
    

7. Digital Knowledge Base
    

- Create a beautiful, searchable archive of insights and stories
    
- Allow members to tag their own experiences to relevant research themes
    
- Build a public-facing component that shares non-sensitive insights with other entrepreneurs and researchers
    

Research Areas:

1. Social Cartography
    

- Map how relationships form and spread throughout the neighborhood
    
- Document the "six degrees" connections that emerge from dinner series
    
- Visualize how community ties strengthen over time
    

3. Norm Evolution
    

- Track how specific behaviors change from first dinner to sixth
    
- Document how norms from structured dinners transfer to daytime interactions
    
- Identify which environmental cues most effectively shape behavior
    

5. Economic Impact
    

- Measure how strengthened community connections impact local spending patterns
    
- Document instances of business collaborations that emerge from connections made
    
- Track changes in customers' relationship to the neighborhood

Over the last 10 years, I’ve lived in communes, ecovillages and co-living spaces, studying how coherence and trust form. Across them all, I saw a recurring pattern: dining together is the single most reliable entry point for bringing strangers into community.  
After many years of community projects, the path seemed obvious – what if we port this pattern to the city? What if a restaurant could rebuild our eroding social and civic infra?  
One of my hopes for the restaurant, then, is that it introduces and inaugurates a new possible “form” of the restaurant, one that is both more profitable and meaningful. If this becomes a part of the ontology of restaurants, it can scale through others, in much the way that “farm-to-table” scaled not through a single operator, but through the proliferation of an idea.  
In a more abstract sense, the restaurant doubles as a “storefront lab”: a prototype for new civic primitives. Most new city projects focus on the macro: zoning, urbanist master plans, and governance. But few interrogate the microstructure – the storefronts and ground-level commercial spaces through which civic life is lived and reproduced. We’re interested in the latter: exploring how the fundamental units of a city can themselves be reimagined as vehicles for learning, belonging, and coordination. I'm conceiving of the restaurant as a composable element of better neighborhoods and cities.  
To achieve this, Garnet must be a place of knowledge production – a restaurant that learns in public and produces open-source documentation, patterns, and tools that others can adopt. Our own “pattern language”. I want to ensure we don’t just rebuild the same commercial typologies and social institutions, but generate new ones capable of sustaining emergent civic life.  
This effort also works on another fronts. The publishing becomes a way for curious individuals to learn more about the restaurant, and to orient their attention before they come in for dinner. Most restaurants just publish their menu and aesthetics, but we want to publish our ideas. For the typical diner, we’ll have more digestible branding and language, but for the curious diner, it becomes a unique invitation to dine and imagine with us.
