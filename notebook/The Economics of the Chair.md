#### **The Spatial Inefficiency of Privacy**

At the restaurant, we had a mix of 2 and 4 tops, and one large communal table. The communal table was hardly used by large groups; we mostly sat groups of two or four, placing a few flowers in between to mark the boundaries. On rare occasion, a large party would book with us and we'd sit them at the communal table. When we did so, that table sat 25-50% more people than it did when it was divided. The table didn't grow in size, but the absence of artificial dead space left more room for seats. Looking out onto the rest of the dining room, I now noticed spatial inefficiencies everywhere. Between every two-top was empty space. These strips of empty space, repeated across the whole restaurant, were sized at about 50% of the width of a two-top table. Tables were set apart to establish frames of privacy. Privacy isn't bad, but it has an economic cost: unused space. 

#### **Full of Parties, and Wanting for People**

A restaurant can be full without being full. On our busiest nights at the restaurant – every Friday and Saturday, and on occasion another day – we regularly held a waitlist of 25-50 people. We didn't have enough room for all those wished to dine with us. Yet, as I scanned the dining room, there were many open chairs. Groups of 3 at table of 4, or a solo diner at a table for 2. These were open chairs at private tables. Of course, you can't seat people at these chairs, and so they sit idle over a whole meal while dozens get turned away from a "full" restaurant. 

If a restaurant has 40 seats, and just 4 seats are left unfilled, the restaurant is only operating at 90% capacity. 

Party fullness does not equal people fullness. 

#### **The Temporal Inefficiency of Choice**

In the previous example, I talked about scanning the dining room as an employee. Let's look at the related vantage point of scanning the room as a potential customer. 

You've almost certainly had the following instance happen: you walk into a mostly empty restaurant and ask for a table. The host looks down at their display, swivels their head and looks quizzically at the dining room, draws their vision back to a display where they make a couple of hurried swipes and taps, before the now expected reply of "yeaaaa, we sadly can't accommodate you this evening".  

Those tables you think are empty, well the first one will be filled in 30 minutes, and that other table, it'll be filled in 60. You can't finish your meal in time for them to reset the table and welcome in the next party, so the seats just sit empty. 

In a restaurant, the same table can account for multiple parties over a single service. That is, if a party sits down at 5:30, we could also book a 7:30 reservation at the same table (or seat a walk-in at that time). So a 4-top could potentially accommodate 8 people over a 4-hour service window. 

However, because the customer has the choice to choose exactly when they come in, often we don't get to turn that table more than once. Maybe the first seating at that 4-top is at 7:00, and you won't be able to accommodate a group of 4 who inquire about a 6pm seating (there isn't enough time) or an 8:00pm (they'd have to wait an hour). 

If the two groups were at 6:00 and 8:00, that'd be great, but it didn't line up that way. So, that one 4-top that can sit 8 over the course of a night, now only sat 4. For that table, we're operating at 50% efficiency.

Some restaurants may get two seatings at the table of four, but the first seating is at 6pm and then next is at 9pm. That restaurant opened at 5pm, so those four seats sat empty for an hour from 5-6pm. The 6pm finished at 8pm, so the seats than sat dormant for another hour until 9pm. The 9pm party finished at 11pm. The restaurants got to turn the table once, but was open for 6 hours, paid their staff for those 6 hours, and only 4 of the 6 hours had buts in the seats.

### The Economics of the Chair

You may think that a restaurant's perishable inventory is its food. It is not. It's the chairs, or more precisely, time in the chairs. If a seat is not occupied for an hour, that seat-hour – the only truly important part of restaurant's inventory - perishes. That empty seat cannot be stored and sold tomorrow. 

The restaurant's seat-hours are the basic unit of its inventory.  

Consider a 40-seat restaurant open from 5pm to 10pm.

At the beginning of the evening, there are 200 available seat-hours (40 seats x 5 hours). 

For a single four-top, there are 20 available seat-hours.

On a given night, that table of 4 may look like the following
- 5pm-6pm: empty
- 6pm-8pm: party of 3
- 8pm-10pm: a party of 4 

By the end of the evening, that table sold 14 of its available 20 seat-hours, or a 70% utilization. 

This is a crude view of a restaurant's economics, but it's a very useful one. It demonstrates how the primary levers have to do with the chair: how many, their utilization, how consistently they are replaced, and so on. 

There are, of course, other levers. A restaurant can increase its check average or get a table out in 90 minutes instead of 120 minutes. But, these all exist within the seat-hour view of the restaurant. 

#### A Different Model

When I offered to help my friend open their restaurant, it was because I was seriously considering my own.

I had conceived of a restaurant with a very basic premise: what if we eat together?

Now imagine a restaurant organized around a slightly different premise: rather than reserving a private table, guests reserve individual chairs at a shared table; rather than arriving whenever they choose, they come for one of a small number of common seating times. Eat together. 

Those two changes are enough to reorganize restaurant’s economics.

##### Booked Seats

Typically, at a large table with open chairs. 

The conventional restaurant may have a dozen scattered open seats and an active waitlist, because they seat private parties at private tables. In this new model, where diners eat together, every chair is individually bookable, so at peak demand the restaurant is truly full. 

If chairs are sold, rather the tables, you never deal with the problem of a trapped chair. 

##### Set Times

Shared tables address the inefficient use of space, and shared times for eating address the inefficient use of time. Since people are eating together, with others they may not know, you may want everyone to begin their meal together. Set seating times ensures that, with sufficient demand, every chair accommodates multiple seatings.

The better argument is that the restaurant has already committed to many costs simply by opening:

- rent;
- insurance;
- salaried or scheduled labor;
- equipment;
- utilities;
- and the basic cost of running a service.

That additional revenue matters because many of the costs of a restaurant have already been committed simply by opening its doors. Rent, insurance, equipment, utilities, and much of the evening’s labor remain largely the same whether a chair is occupied or empty. Revenue from otherwise stranded chairs can therefore make a disproportionate contribution toward paying those costs.

This is not merely a question of increasing profits. A restaurant with stronger underlying economics has greater capacity to pay its employees well, absorb a difficult month, invest in its space and food, and remain open long enough to become meaningful to the people around it.
