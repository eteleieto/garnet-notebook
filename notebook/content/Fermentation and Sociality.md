From soy sauce to vinegar to cheeses to breads to wines, our favorite and keystone foodstuffs are fermented. Fermentation also helps upcycle food scraps and minimize kitchen waste. 

Yet, there is another important dimensions to fermentation, one intimately related to the social mission of Garnet, and thats ... sociality....

#### Ferments and Conversation

Social acts are embedded in the practice of making and sharing ferments. 

At communal tables, amongst mostly strangers, we're trying to bring people out into social space. 

There are many such ways to do this, but one method is to introduce new flavors. 

Have you ... "what is that?", "I've never had that flavor before", "it's kind of like this or like this..."

When we try something new, taste an unknown flavor, we turn to others to figure what that flavor is. Each person, having ingested the same thing, seeks out the other. The aim is not to explicitly challenge one's preferences, but to introduce a bit of unfamiliar space in the territory. Confronted with the unknown and the ill-defined, conversation is the exploratory tool. 


At the restaurant, as we develop [[Regional Food Culture]], ferments will be our frequented partner in developing these novel ingredients. 

Thus, ferments are the stimulus, that bubble into conversation.  

#### Historical Significance

Historically, across the Northeast US, and in much of the world, ferment-ing was a way of extending the abundance of the growing season through the colder months. 

- that abundance, and the processing required to store it, was done collectively. 
- Take sauerkraut. Families and neighbors would gather to chop, salt, and jar many, many cabbages. This salty and lactic-y cabbage fed whole families and neighborhoods, especially before the advent of refrigeration. People fermented in bulk. The act of fermenting was a communal act. We cannot understand the history of ferments without speaking of it as a gathering and method of collective resilience. 

Though the act today does not always align with its collectivist origins, the consumption of ferments involves ourselves in a fundamentally social history.




#### Trust

[[Joshua Evans]] describes fermentation as "culturally appropriate, socially significant, carefully controlled rot."

That it rots properly requires trust in the person or people who made it.

This is a restaurant that principally cares about generating belonging. Part of that is developing human relations, through the age old civic tradition of eating with your neighbors (see [[Commensalism]]). 

Yet, another part, often under-acknowledged, is one's relation to place and region. 

I believe eating is one of our best available entrances into this relation. 

We know this all intuitively when we travel. You go somewhere, and you learn about that place through food. You enter a stranger's home, and they welcome you with their region's food. 

Regional food is the language of a place, a fundamental and foundational unit of culture.

Yet, most of the food we eat, shaped by shelf stability and scale, is trucked from thousand of miles away, nutritionally hollow, flavorless, and aggressively processed. 

I think this leads to profound spiritual alienation. Our food are totally unspecific, a food system of [[nowhere places | nowhere in particular]]. As such, we also have a narrowing of culinary, botanical, and cultural diversity. 

A goal of the restaurant is to develop our regional food culture [[Regional Food Culture]], for taste and belonging (among many other reasons). 

The road away from alienation is through coming to know the food of this region, of your region, alongside the people with whom you live near. When you feel like you belong to a place, you start to care for it. This is how you go from sitting down for a meal with strangers to a vibrant local civics.
